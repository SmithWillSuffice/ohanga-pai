[here, “NZ Gov has run out of money”](https://neweconomicmanagement.substack.com/p/the-new-zealand-government-has-run/).


**Maurizio Piglia wrote:** <br>
Masterful article . The article’s most valuable sentence, analytically, is 
its final reframing: <br>
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;_Stop asking “How will you pay for it?” Ask “What resources are required, and can the economy provide them?”_ <br>
We should add, in my opinion, just one further question: <br>
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;_"Who receives the income, credit and productive capacity created by the spending?"_ <br>
That is the missing distributional lens. But I'm sure you thought about it.


**Jess Berentson-Shaw wrote:** <br>
I continue to ponder how on earth to tell this story in a way that is easy 
to hear and share….. my normal process for developing new narratives and 
frames for complex issues requires I first really understand the issues, 
and sadly some of this stuff still alludes me! I shall continue to ponder

I do know that if you show people any account going into negative/debt 
they will interpret this as a bad thing because of the cultural mental 
models we have about how money works. For example even though the point is 
we went into negative and we’re still able to cover costs, the 
negative = bad. So wonder if there is a way to frame this differently….

**Bijou wrote (in reply to Jess):**

> Do not do that. Reject and resist that framing with all your intellectual 
might. Do not let it pass, even at the polite Christmas/Matariki dinner 
table with your neolib and neocon rellies. On an accounting ledger there are 
no negative signs. The debt is on one side, the credit on the other. But 
there are quadruple entries, so your debt is always some other account's 
credit.

And do not be the first to leave the dinner table. Let them leave first. 
You never want to waste the roast carrots, kumura, and turkey stuffing 
and cranberry sauce.

The issue is not the negative connotation, but why people forget about the 
other ledger. Do they want the nongovernment sector to be in net debt, and 
only the currency issuer in net credit in Its own scorepoints? That 
reveals the clown show of the Monetarists and essentially all mainstream 
economists, sadly. Even many so-called Marxists.

You can also be “fair” and point out any government borrowing a 
foreign currency is indeed borrowing, but if they have a non-zero fx rate 
it is still of no 𝘰𝘱𝘦𝘳𝘢𝘵𝘪𝘰𝘯𝘢𝘭 𝘤𝘢𝘱𝘢𝘤𝘪𝘵𝘺 consequence, since their scorepoints 
can be swapped for the other nation's scorepoints, so modulo domestic 
fiscal adjustment (to ensure full employment after the foreign trade 
sector rebalancing) their foreign debt is again largely irrelevant (a 
psychological mental health problem, not a fiscal crisis problem. Why 
borrow foreign scorepoints in the first place? It'd only because they 
were ꕷꖡꖹꘝꕯꕒ or duped or both.)
